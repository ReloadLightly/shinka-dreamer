# GLOBAL INSIGHTS SCRATCHPAD

The following are global insights about optimization approaches and their effectiveness:

## Successful Algorithmic Patterns
- **Generation 4, Adaptive Enemy Mixture with Risk-Aware Receding-Horizon Planning, is the strongest evaluated program:** task fitness **0.9509**, 95.83% escape, zero deaths, and 65.78 steps per escape. Its successful combination includes uniform/slow/fast motion experts, collision-aware anonymous inference, risk-weighted routing, and three-step planning. This combination outperformed every supplied alternative; the evaluations do not isolate individual components’ contributions.
- Adaptive motion models paired with straightforward routing performed well. **Generation 1, Adaptive Enemy Prediction**, scored **0.917**, versus **0.799** for Generation 0’s cumulative occupancy learner, while escape increased from 77.8% to 91.7% and death fell from 20.8% to 6.9%. The improved program also changed inference and forecasting, so this comparison does not establish that forgetting alone caused the gains.
- The best program achieved a stronger safety/completion balance than longer-horizon variants. Compared with **Generation 3, Anonymous Motion Learning** (**0.898**), Generation 4 increased escape from 90.3% to 95.83%, eliminated observed deaths, and shortened successful escapes from 95.6 to 65.78 steps.
## Ineffective Approaches
- **Generation 6, Anonymous Dynamics Learning with Survival-Conditioned Beam Planning**, had the weakest completion efficiency: **0.7802** fitness, 76.4% escape, 15.3% timeout, and 132.8 steps per escape. Its four-step survival-conditioned beam planner coincided with roughly twice the best program’s escape duration, while still suffering 8.3% deaths. More elaborate path-conditioned planning did not deliver better observed safety or progress.
- **Generation 2, Bayesian Enemy Dynamics**, combined 113 movement-law hypotheses with five-step planning but scored **0.8703**, below Generation 1’s **0.917** and Generation 4’s **0.9509**. Its 8.3% timeout rate and 73.61-step successful escapes show that this additional modeling and planning complexity did not translate into stronger completion.
- **Generation 5, Adaptive Enemy Motion Learning**, delivered the fastest reported successful escapes at **52.64 steps**, but fitness was only **0.891**, with 88.9% escape and 8.3% death. Its one-step forecast, Dijkstra routing, and risk-based waiting traded observed reliability for speed relative to Generation 4. Successful-escape duration also excludes failed episodes, limiting direct efficiency comparisons.
## Implementation Insights
- The best program explicitly handles **blocked attempts, anonymous occupancy, and censored observations**. `_terrain_destination` redirects blocked movement to the origin; `_infer_anonymous` sums assignments whose occupied-cell union matches observations, allowing overlaps. `_learn` downweights directionally uninformative posteriors and skips transitions across scheduled terrain changes. These mechanisms avoid treating blocked movement or changing geometry as straightforward evidence of an enemy’s preferred direction.
- Generation 4 combines complementary adaptation mechanisms: slow counts retain **0.985** per update, fast counts **0.86**, and likelihood-weighted experts include a fixed uniform model. Its final movement law retains **12% uniform probability mass**. This provides both adaptation and smoothing; its strong switching escape rate of **95.83%** is consistent with robust behavior, but does not independently demonstrate adaptation.
- The planner distinguishes **future occupancy from collision hazard**. `_forecast` combines enemy presence before and after movement, subtracting their overlap through stay probability, and includes diffuse unseen-enemy intensity. Planning penalizes `-log(1-hazard)`, excludes immediate destinations containing visible enemies, and discounts later hazards across three steps. These are concrete safety mechanisms in the zero-observed-death result, although their separate effects remain unmeasured.
- Mapping and target selection support sustained progress: displacement-based localization preserves terrain memory, frontier scores balance travel cost against newly observable area, and an **18% target-retention tolerance** reduces frontier switching. Timestamps are stored, but the best program’s frontier gain measures unseen cells rather than stale terrain; timestamps alone do not constitute adaptive map-based exploration.
## Performance Analysis
- Generation 4’s **0.9509** fitness exceeds Generation 1 by **0.0339**, Generation 7’s multiscale two-step planner (**0.9138**) by **0.0371**, and Generation 6 by **0.1707**. Neither deeper planning nor survival conditioning shows a consistently positive association with fitness across these evaluations.
- The best program escaped in **69/72 episodes**, with three timeouts and no deaths: **24/24 uniform**, **22/24 stationary**, and **23/24 switching**. Stationary dynamics were also the weakest regime for Generations 0, 1, 3, 5, and 7. Generation 4 substantially narrows this recurring weakness, but each regime contains only 24 episodes.
- Generation 4’s near-cell Brier loss was **0.00761 versus 0.01134** for the supplied baseline, approximately **33% lower**; audit loss was **0.01539 versus 0.01671**, approximately **8% lower**. Sparse audit occupancy limits interpretation. Generation 6 had still lower near-cell loss (**0.00293**) alongside much worse task fitness, illustrating that prediction diagnostics across differing agent trajectories do not establish control benefit.
- Switching evidence is limited but concrete: **21/24** switching episodes encountered a switch, contributing **701 post-switch steps**, including **340 with visible enemies**. Post-switch audit loss was lower than pre-switch loss, while near-cell loss was slightly higher. Without comparative intervention outcomes and uncertainty estimates, these aggregates establish neither adaptive-learning gains nor a causal contribution to escape performance.

# META RECOMMENDATIONS

The following are actionable recommendations for the next program generations:

1. **Add targeted refresh of stale route bottlenecks.** Extend `_choose_target` with observation targets that reveal old walls or disconnected corridors near remembered doors and exits, using `m["seen"]` and the terrain-change schedule. Activate this when objectives appear unreachable or progress stalls; the best program already collects two keys per episode on average, so reconnecting routes is a promising way to address its remaining timeouts without replacing successful frontier exploration.
2. **Maintain enemy beliefs across visibility loss.** Replace the freshly initialized diffuse hidden intensity in `_forecast` with a persistent spatial belief propagated through the learned movement kernel, removing mass from currently observed empty cells and reconciling visible occupancy without assuming enemy identities. Preserve blocked-attempt behavior and separate occupancy from collision hazard; this extends the best program’s successful forecasting while potentially reducing both blind spots and unnecessary avoidance around recently cleared areas.
3. **Make fast-expert recovery respond to predictive surprise.** On valid, informative transitions, accumulate evidence that the fast or uniform expert predicts anonymous observations better than the slow expert; sustained evidence can trigger a fresh short-memory expert while retaining the slow model. Keep the uniform probability floor and terrain-change exclusions, and normalize evidence for the number of observed enemies so crowd size alone does not trigger recovery. This builds on the winning mixture while directly targeting adaptation after movement-law switches.
4. **Introduce a bounded stall-recovery controller.** Track repeated position–target cycles and steps since a key pickup, door opening, or meaningful route advance; when stalled, compare alternate approach routes and observation waypoints before choosing further waits. Retain the three-step planner and immediate visible-enemy exclusion, but selectively reduce repeated-wait preference when a low-hazard detour exists. The remaining failures are timeouts, whereas deeper survival-conditioned planning previously increased duration substantially.
5. **Plan with expert disagreement instead of only the averaged movement law.** Forecast hazards separately under the uniform, slow, and fast experts, then score candidate actions using expected collision cost plus an uncertainty penalty restricted to plausible experts that disagree near the proposed route. Apply this selectively around nearby threats and retain the successful three-step horizon to limit computation and excess caution. This extends the mixture’s safety mechanisms while testing whether uncertainty-aware decisions improve reliability beyond averaging alone.
